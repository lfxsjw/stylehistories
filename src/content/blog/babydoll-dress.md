There is a dress that has spent seven decades pretending to be the most innocent thing in a woman's closet. It is short. It is loose. It has a waist that sits where a nightgown's should, high under the bust, and a skirt that flares out before it has to do any real work of containing anything. Fashion calls it the babydoll dress, and every few years it comes back, always sold with the same story: sweet, youthful, a little bit of girlhood you can put on.

Here is the thing. That story is a lie, and it has been a lie since the beginning. The babydoll dress was never innocent. It was born out of a wartime shortage, named by a movie so scandalous that a Catholic cardinal went on the radio to condemn it, and worn by every generation of women precisely *because* it let them play with the line between child and woman without saying a word. Its innocence is the most calculated thing about it.

## A nightgown cut short to save fabric

The dress started as underwear, which is the first thing everyone gets wrong about it. In 1942, the American lingerie designer Sylvia Pedlar was running Iris Lingerie in New York and staring at the same problem every other manufacturer faced: the War Production Board was rationing fabric. You could not make a full-length silk nightgown anymore, not if you wanted to stay in business.

So Pedlar did what a resourceful designer does. She cut the nightgown short, right up to the upper thigh, and fitted the bodice so it ended in a seam just under the bust, with the fabric falling away from there. Less cloth, same customer, no complaint from the ration board. She also made a version for small children, kept short so a diaper could be changed without wrestling the whole garment over a baby's head.

It is worth pausing on how unglamorous this origin is. The babydoll, the garment that would become shorthand for a particular kind of flirtation, began as a cost-cutting measure. And Pedlar, the woman who actually made it, hated what people eventually called it. When the name "babydoll" stuck, she refused to use it. The woman who invented the thing wanted nothing to do with the label the culture pasted onto it. That small refusal turns out to be the whole story in miniature: the babydoll has always belonged less to the people who made it than to the people who saw it and decided what it meant.

## A name borrowed from a scandal

The name did not come from Pedlar. It came from a movie, and the movie is where the innocence story collapses for good.

In 1956, the director Elia Kazan released *Baby Doll*, adapted from a Tennessee Williams play, with Carroll Baker in the title role as a nineteen-year-old bride who still sleeps in a crib, sucks her thumb, and wears a short, loose nightgown. The film was a scandal before it opened. Francis Spellman, the Cardinal Archbishop of New York, condemned it from the pulpit of St. Patrick's Cathedral and told Catholics not to see it. His objection was not subtle: here was a grown woman, dressed and behaving like a child, and the camera lingered on her. The Legion of Decency, the Catholic ratings board, weighed in. Newspapers debated it for weeks.

None of that hurt the film. It did the opposite. And somewhere in the noise, the short nightgown Baker wore stopped being "a short nightgown" and became "a babydoll." A garment that had been a pragmatic answer to rationing was now permanently attached to a specific, uncomfortable fantasy: the woman as child, the child as something to look at.

That is the real origin of the name, and it is why the dress's sweetness has never been simple. From the first time the word appeared, "babydoll" meant innocence and something else at the same time. It meant the thing you were not supposed to want, dressed up in ribbons.

## From the boudoir to Carnaby Street

For a decade the babydoll stayed where Pedlar had put it, in the bedroom. Then the 1960s got ahold of it and dragged it out onto the street, which is where the story takes its strangest turn.

The shift began in the couture houses. Cristóbal Balenciaga was already obsessed with cutting the waistline out of fashion, and in 1957 and 1958 he put the babydoll's raised seam and loose skirt into his collections. He liked the silhouette for the same reason Pedlar had settled on it, though he would have put it differently: it hides the waist, blurs the proportions, turns the body into a shape rather than an hourglass.

But it was not Balenciaga who made the babydoll famous. It was London. In the mid-sixties, the mod scene around Carnaby Street turned a short, shapeless A-line frock into a uniform for girls who were done dressing like their mothers. Twiggy wore it, all legs and lashes. Pattie Boyd and Jean Shrimpton wore it. Jane Birkin wore it with colored tights and a basket. The dress was called the "dolly girl" look in the press, and the name was not a coincidence: these women were borrowing the visual language of childhood, the Peter Pan collars and puff sleeves and pastel gingham, and wearing it as a provocation.

Here is the twist that keeps getting lost. The miniskirt was already scandalous in 1965. A grown woman wearing a dress that looked like a toddler's nightie was worse, because it was not just short, it was *knowing*. It took the exact object the Cardinal had condemned a decade earlier, the woman-as-child, and put it in the daylight, on the street, worn by women who had chosen it themselves. The dolly girls did not invent the babydoll's ambiguity. They inherited it from Carroll Baker and they ran with it.

## Grunge took it and flipped it again

Every generation that adopts the babydoll has to decide which half of its meaning to lean on, and the nineties made the most aggressive choice of all.

Courtney Love wore babydoll dresses with ripped stockings and Doc Martens. Kat Bjelland of Babes in Toyland wore them too, and the Riot Grrrl scene around them turned the look into a named thing: "kinderwhore," a deliberate collision of childlike sweetness and everything that contradicted it. A baby-blue dress over torn fishnets and combat boots. A hair ribbon next to smeared lipstick. The point was the clash itself. These women were not trying to look innocent. They were holding the dress up to the light and forcing you to see what it had always contained.

The fashion industry noticed. Anna Sui put a version of it on the runway in 1994. Marc Jacobs did his own take, and by the end of the decade the babydoll was back in department stores, this time sold to teenagers as a bit of nineties cool. What had started as a subculture's refusal to be pretty became, almost instantly, something you could buy at the mall. That is the babydoll's other constant: it gets radicalized, and then it gets absorbed, and neither process ever quite finishes.

## Why it keeps coming back

You could read all of this as a tidy little history and stop there. But the reason the babydoll refuses to stay dead is the reason it is worth writing about at all.

It comes back in 2026 the way it always does, on runways and then in stores. Chloé, under Chemena Kamali, has leaned into a sheer, romantic version of it. Miu Miu keeps putting it out with a wink. McQueen has reworked the shape. The trend coverage says the same thing it said in 1965 and 1994: the babydoll is back, it is sweet, it is playful, it is girlish. And the coverage is wrong in the same way, every time, because the dress has never been one thing. It has always been two things at once, held in tension.

That tension is the dress. Pedlar made it to save cloth, and it became a fetish object. A cardinal condemned it, and it became a bestseller. Women wore it on Carnaby Street to reject being looked at, and it got them looked at more. Courtney Love wore it to shatter the fantasy, and the fantasy survived her. A garment this old and this simple should have settled into one meaning decades ago. It never has, and it shows no sign of starting now.

So when the babydoll dress comes back around, as it is doing right now, the honest thing to say is not "isn't it sweet." The honest thing is that this dress has spent seventy years smuggling the least innocent questions in fashion, the ones about who gets to be a child and who gets to be a woman and who is doing the looking, past every censor it has ever met. It is short, and it is loose, and it is not hiding anything. It never was.
