The most expensive sweater ever sold at auction is a green cardigan with a cigarette burn in it and a button missing. Kurt Cobain wore it once, for Nirvana's MTV *Unplugged* set in November 1993, and never washed it again. In October 2019 it sold for $334,000. Nobody bid on it because of the wool. They bid on it because of who wore it, and because of the strange thing a cardigan can mean when it hangs off the right shoulders.

Here is the part that gets left out of that story. The cardigan is named after a real man, James Brudenell, the 7th Earl of Cardigan, and almost everything about him is a warning about vanity. He was a British aristocrat who bought his way into command of a cavalry regiment, spent a fortune on uniforms, and led his men into the single most famous military blunder of the nineteenth century. The garment that carries his name survived him. The question is how a piece of clothing managed to escape a man this embarrassing.

## A name earned in the wrong place

The Battle of Balaclava took place on 25 October 1854, during the Crimean War. Lord Cardigan, as he was styled, led the Charge of the Light Brigade: roughly six hundred cavalrymen sent straight into the mouths of Russian guns because of a muddled order. It accomplished nothing. Tennyson turned it into a poem about obedience, which made it look noble, but the charge itself was a disaster. A large share of the brigade was killed or wounded in about twenty minutes.

Before all that, in the cold of the Crimean winter, Cardigan wore a knitted wool waistcoat that opened down the front with buttons. The point was practical: he could put it on and take it off without pulling it over his head and wrecking the careful arrangement of a cavalry officer's hair and collar. That is the whole, unglamorous origin. A man who cared enormously about how he looked wanted a warm layer that would not disturb the look.

The story you sometimes hear, that he slashed his sweater open with a sword mid-battle, is almost certainly invented. It is a good story and probably not true. What is true is that the buttoned wool waistcoat became attached to his name, and after he returned to London a minor celebrity, the "cardigan" became a fashionable thing for men to wear.

## How the cardigan got away from him

That version of the story — manly, military, named for a soldier — is not where the cardigan lived. It is where it was born. The people who actually made the cardigan matter were women.

In the 1920s, Coco Chanel took the buttoned knit and made it a woman's garment. She had a concrete, slightly petty reason: she disliked pullover sweaters because pulling them over her head ruined her hair. A cardigan you could button avoided the problem entirely. So she wore them, and sold them, in soft jersey, cut to hang loosely off the body rather than cinch it. This was not a designer having a grand idea about liberation. It was a practical woman solving an annoyance, and the solution happened to look modern.

What Chanel started, decades of women finished. The cardigan became the "twinset" of the 1950s, the matching knit and short-sleeve jumper worn with pearls and a certain kind of composure. It became the uniform of a woman who had nothing left to prove, which is a strange afterlife for something named after a man who clearly had everything to prove.

## The two hundred years in between

The cardigan never really went away. It just kept changing hands, which is why it has meant so many contradictory things.

Fred Rogers changed into a cardigan at the start of every episode of *Mister Rogers' Neighborhood*, and the gesture became a signal that it was time to be gentle. Queen Elizabeth II wore them for decades, often in bright colors, a knit that read as steady and unbothered. Then grunge took the same garment and made it mean the opposite. Kurt Cobain's thrifted, oversized, slightly grubby cardigans looked like they had been picked off a dead man, which in a sense was the point. The same buttoned front that once said "composure" now said "I have stopped trying."

Pop culture keeps finding new ways to re-litigate the thing. In 2020 Taylor Swift released a song called "Cardigan," using the garment as a stand-in for a relationship you keep returning to. The same year Harry Styles wore a patchwork cardigan by JW Anderson on a TV appearance, and TikTok spent months recreating it; the Victoria and Albert Museum in London later acquired the piece for its collection. A 2020s TikToker knitting a patchwork cardigan is, at several removes, still doing what a Crimean cavalry officer did: making a buttoned knit that says something about the person wearing it.

## What I actually make of it

I keep coming back to one detail. The cardigan is named for a specific person, which almost no garment is. There is no "the Wellington" you pull on every day, no "the Blücher" in your closet. Names get attached to boots and jackets sometimes, but they usually fade into generic words. The cardigan is the exception: it has kept its founder's name for a hundred and seventy years while abandoning everything else about him.

I think that is because the cardigan is fundamentally a private garment. It is the thing you put on at home, over your shoulders when the evening cools, the layer that is about how you feel rather than how you are seen. A man who cared only about how he was seen is a strange person to be its namesake. Maybe that is the real story: the cardigan had to be rescued from its own inventor, by people — mostly women, mostly at home — who understood what it was actually for.

That is why Cobain's unwashed cardigan could sell for $334,000 while still missing a button. The value was never in the garment. It was in the fact that a buttoned wool knit, invented so a vain aristocrat could keep his hair tidy in a war zone, had become the thing a grieving, exhausted man reached for on the most honest night of his career. The cardigan holds up because it keeps getting taken away from people who want to be impressive and given to people who just want to be comfortable.

Next time you button one, think about whose name is on it. The man who gave it the name is barely worth remembering. The garment, like always, found its way to better company.
