No single garment has traveled further than the blue jean. Born on the American frontier in the 1870s, it now hangs in the closets of miners and movie stars, presidents and punk rockers. It is, quite possibly, the most democratic piece of clothing ever invented.

## The birth of denim

The story begins in San Francisco in 1873. A tailor named Jacob Davis had a problem: the work pants he sold to Gold Rush miners kept tearing at the pockets. His solution was simple and brilliant — he reinforced the stress points with copper rivets.

Davis did not have the money to patent the idea, so he wrote to his fabric supplier, a Bavarian immigrant named Levi Strauss. Strauss agreed to fund the patent. On May 20, 1873, the blue jean was born.

The fabric was denim, a sturdy cotton twill, and the color was indigo blue — chosen because it hid dirt. The pants were built to be worked in, not admired.

## From workwear to rebellion

For the next seventy years, jeans stayed in the mines, the ranches, and the factories. They were the uniform of honest labor.

Then came the 1950s. Hollywood put denim on the screen — Marlon Brando in *The Wild One*, James Dean in *Rebel Without a Cause*. Almost overnight, jeans became the uniform of teenage rebellion. Schools banned them. Parents worried about them. Which, of course, only made teenagers want them more.

The 1960s and 70s completed the transformation. Jeans became a canvas for protest, for counterculture, for self-expression. They were embroidered, patched, painted, and worn until they fell apart.

## The high-fashion takeover

In 1980, Calvin Klein put a fifteen-year-old Brooke Shields in its designer jeans and asked, on camera, what came between her and her Calvins. "Nothing," she answered. The Federal Communications Commission investigated. Nothing was ever the same.

Designer denim was born. The riveted five-pocket design that had come out of a San Francisco workshop now sold for hundreds of dollars, and every major fashion house followed.

Today, a pair of jeans sells somewhere in the world every single second. They are worn by billionaires and by the people who build their houses, by world leaders and by the workers who clean their offices.

## Why jeans endure

The blue jean survives because it means everything and nothing at once. It has meant workwear and luxury, conformity and rebellion — sometimes in the same decade.

And it all started with a practical tailor and a pair of riveted pockets in 1873.
