In 1964, a French designer named André Courrèges put a woman in a white dress on a Paris runway and put white boots on her feet, and the point of the whole exercise was that none of it should remind you of anything. He was tired of looking back. He had trained at Balenciaga, where he learned to cut a jacket until it stood up by itself, and by the early sixties he had decided that fashion was moving too slowly for the life people actually lived. "We no longer walk through life," he said around then. "We run, we dance. You drive a car, you take a plane. Your clothes have to be able to move too."

So he designed the boot to move. Mid-calf, flat or nearly flat, stripped of the pointed toe and the thin stiletto that had defined a decade of ladylike shoes. The boot he sent down the runway was white, which made it read as clean and a little surgical, like something a cosmonaut might wear to a party. He called the look the Moon Girl, and the fashion press filed it under a new label: Space Age. The boots got a name of their own. First they were Courrèges boots, then, somewhere between the runway and the dance floor, they became go-go boots, and the name stuck.

Here is the strange thing, and it took sixty years to become obvious. Courrèges made that boot to escape the past. He wanted a shoe with no ancestry, a shoe that pointed only forward. And the world took it and did the opposite. The go-go boot became one of the most reliable ways fashion has ever found to look backward. Every decade or so, when a designer wants to conjure up the sixties in a single object, they reach for the white boot. The boot built to mean "the future" is now the fastest shorthand we have for a specific, finished past. Nothing else in fashion carries that contradiction so cleanly.

The name is its own little history, and a good one. "Go-go" traces back to the French *à gogo*, meaning in abundance, and from there to a 1962 American slang use of "go" for anything all the rage. Then, in January 1964, a club called the Whisky a Go Go opened on the Sunset Strip in West Hollywood, and the dancers who worked in cages above the crowd in short skirts and white boots gave the term a body. A "go-go dancer" hit print in 1965, and the boot inherited the name from the dance. Which is fitting, because the boot was always more about moving than about sitting still.

What the boot actually did, culturally, is worth being precise about, because it gets flattened into "sixties fun" and that undersells it. Before the go-go boot, a woman's boot was weather gear. Boots were for rain, for snow, for riding, not for walking down the street in sunshine. The idea that a boot could be a fashion shoe, worn to the office or the grocery store, was genuinely new. A South Carolina shoe buyer told a local paper in 1970 that boots had "moved out of the mud puddle into the mainstream," sold as often in good weather as bad. That is a bigger shift than it sounds. The boot took an object associated with utility and discomfort and made it a statement. It also, not incidentally, offered coverage to women who wanted shorter hemlines but not the full leg on display. A mini skirt and a pair of white boots was a way to be daring and modest at the same time, which is part of why the look spread so fast through teenagers and "dolly birds" in London.

Courrèges was not alone, and the boot's origin is genuinely contested. The American shoe company Golo claimed to have invented it the same year, and got a serious publicity boost when Barbra Streisand wore a pair in a Richard Avedon shoot for the August 1965 issue of Vogue. Whoever got there first, both versions shared the same idea: a low heel, a clean white finish, and a square or rounded toe that looked nothing like the pointed pumps of the fifties. Courrèges, to his credit, never much cared about the copycats. His boot was simple enough to be reproduced cheaply, and it was, in PVC and vinyl and every color under the sun.

What locked the boot into permanent memory was not a runway. It was a song. In 1966 Nancy Sinatra released "These Boots Are Made for Walkin'," written by Lee Hazlewood, and it went to number one while she performed it in white boots, the title turned into a threat. The song did something a fashion collection rarely manages: it attached a feeling to the object. Tim Gunn has said the boot became, in Sinatra's hands, a symbol of female power. That is not overstatement. A boot that started as a design experiment about movement ended up as a lyric about a woman walking out on a man who did her wrong, and the two meanings fused. The go-go boot was no longer just a shoe. It was a posture.

Which is exactly why it keeps coming back, and why the current revival is worth noticing. In 2025, Miuccia Prada put a go-go boot with a small buckle down her autumn Miu Miu runway, and the thing sold out at close to eighteen hundred euros while the internet argued about it. Courrèges itself, now run by a new creative director, sent its own version down the spring 2026 runway, a direct reference to the house's founding silhouette. Chloé's Janis boot and a shelf of fast-fashion knockoffs followed. Every time this happens, the framing is roughly the same: the boot is back, and it is back as nostalgia.

That framing is the whole problem, and it is the reason this boot is more interesting than it looks. A nostalgia object trades on comfort, on warmth, on the fuzzy recollection of a time the wearer may not even have lived through. But there is nothing comfortable about a white go-go boot. It is vinyl and hard edges and a kind of deliberate flatness. It was designed to be cold and future-facing, and it still reads that way. What actually happens when a designer revives it is not that people get cozy. It is that a new generation borrows a bit of borrowed confidence from a decade they half-remember, and the boot works as a prop for that borrowing. It is nostalgia as a power move rather than a blanket. That is a different animal from a peasant blouse coming back.

So the go-go boot sits in a strange double life. It is, at once, one of the most futuristic things ever put on a foot and one of the most retro. Courrèges made it to mean "no past," and we now use it to mean "the sixties." The design won and lost at the same time. It won, in that it still looks like nothing else and still gets dragged out every few years by people who want to feel like they are moving faster than everyone else. It lost, in that the future it was built for never arrived, and the boot got reassigned to the past instead.

There is a version of this where you read the whole thing as failure. A designer reaches for tomorrow and the culture hands him a costume. But I think that misses what the boot still does, which is the thing it was built to do. Put a pair on and you are not blending in. You are signaling, loudly, that you are in motion. The go-go boot never stopped meaning "go." It just stopped meaning "forward." Same word, sixty years apart, pointed in two directions at once.

The next time you see a white boot on a runway or on someone on the street, that is the whole story in one object. It was built to escape the past and became the past's favorite mirror. A designer gave it to the future, and the future gave it back.
