Double 11 began as a joke — Singles' Day, four lonely number ones, invented by Chinese university students in the nineties as an anti-Valentine's — and grew into the biggest shopping event on the planet. Hong Kong's version of it has its own accent. At ITeSHOP, the online store of the I.T fashion group, the FW26 sale runs through November 12, and the mechanic is blunt: buy two pieces, get 30% off both.

## The store behind the sale

If you have never heard of I.T, some context. In 1988, Sham Kar Wai and his brother opened a small shop in Causeway Bay called Green Peace, selling British imports to a city that was, back then, hungry for exactly that. The shop outgrew its name, its name outgrew a certain environmental charity's lawyers, and I.T became one of Asia's largest multi-brand fashion groups — parent to homegrown labels like b+ab, IZZUE, 5cm and :CHOCOOLATE, and the regional gatekeeper for Japanese and American brands that Hong Kong shoppers couldn't find anywhere else. ITeSHOP is the group's official web store. Prices are in Hong Kong dollars; delivery is free within Hong Kong on orders over HK$300.

The sale itself — FW26 Double 11, Part 2 — runs November 2 to 12. The rules, as printed on the product pages: two pieces get you 30% off (a smaller "selected pieces" tier, including the Samba below, gets 20%), and if your discounted total clears HK$1,500, the code **26D11** takes another 5% off, while quota lasts. The offer applies to participating items only. With that sorted, here is what I would actually buy — seven pieces from the women's sale section, each with a real history behind it, cheapest first.

## KANGOL's kangaroo tote — HK$299 (about US$38)

  <img src="/images/iteshop-kangol-tote.jpg" alt="Black canvas tote bag with white KANGOL kangaroo print" />

</figure>

KANGOL was founded in 1938 in Cleator, a mill town in the far northwest of England, and spent its first decades making wool caps — including berets for Allied forces during the Second World War. The brand's second life came from an unexpected direction: 1980s New York, where LL Cool J and a generation of rappers turned the bucket hats into hip-hop uniform. The kangaroo logo travelled with them. Here it sits, oversized in white print, on a plain black canvas tote — the single cheapest piece of substance in this sale. Two of these, or one plus anything else in the 30%-off tier, and the arithmetic starts working in your favour.

## Free People's snap-button cardigan — HK$379 (about US$49)

<figure>
  <img src="/images/iteshop-free-people-cardigan.jpg" alt="Black ribbed knit V-neck cardigan with metal snap buttons" />
  <figcaption>Open, it's a cardigan. Snapped shut, it's a top. Free People, in one garment.</figcaption>

The Free People name is older than most of its customers realise. In 1970, Dick Hayne opened a shop in West Philadelphia called Free People, selling secondhand clothes and bohemian pieces to students. That shop grew up, changed its name to Urban Outfitters, and two decades later the founders revived Free People as its own label — the flowy, embroidered, festival-leaning sibling in the family. This black V-neck cardigan is the useful end of that DNA: fine ribbed knit, a column of metal snap buttons, cut close. Worn open it's a layer; snapped shut it's a top. At 30% off in a two-piece basket, it lands around US$35.

## :CHOCOOLATE's Breton stripes — HK$399 (about US$51)

<figure>
  <img src="/images/iteshop-chocooolate-stripe-tee.jpg" alt="Burgundy long-sleeve T-shirt with white horizontal stripes" />
  <figcaption>Burgundy instead of navy: the French naval stripe, re-tuned for autumn.</figcaption>
</figure>

In 1858, the French Navy made the marinière official: a knitted shirt with blue stripes on a white ground, designed so a sailor overboard could be spotted from the deck. The stripe count and spacing were regulated like everything else in the navy. Half a century later, Coco Chanel noticed the shirts on Breton fishermen and holidaymakers and put the pattern into women's fashion, where it has stayed ever since. :CHOCOOLATE, one of I.T's homegrown labels, applies the formula in burgundy and white — same geometry, warmer register. A long-sleeve striped tee at HK$399 is the kind of basic that disappears into a wardrobe and never leaves.

## MURUA's leopard-print zip knit — HK$599 (about US$77)

<figure>
  <img src="/images/iteshop-murua-leopard-knit.jpg" alt="Dusty blue fuzzy knit sweater with leopard print and half-zip collar" />
  <figcaption>Leopard print on a dusty blue ground — the pattern with a century of history, in a quieter key.</figcaption>
</figure>

Leopard print entered high fashion in the 1920s, when Paris houses put it on silk for clients who wanted to look expensive and slightly dangerous. Hollywood did the rest: by the forties and fifties, a woman in leopard print on screen was never the one who died of natural causes. The pattern has returned every winter since, somewhere between cliché and heirloom. MURUA, a Tokyo womenswear label, does a softened version here — leopard woven into a fuzzy, dusty-blue knit, half-zip collar, cropped and loose. On anyone who fears full-cream leopard, the blue ground reads as texture first, animal second.

## The adidas Samba — HK$899 (about US$115)

<figure>
  <img src="/images/iteshop-adidas-samba.jpg" alt="Black adidas Samba sneaker with white stripes and gum sole" />
  <figcaption>Black leather, gum sole: the Samba in its most permanent colourway.</figcaption>
</figure>

Adi Dassler designed the Samba in 1950 as a training shoe for footballers who had to practise on frozen pitches — the gum rubber sole was for grip on hard, cold ground, and the name was a nod to Brazil, host of that year's World Cup. It never went out of production in the seventy-six years since. The shoe moved from touchlines to European terrace culture to, eventually, every high street in the world, and in recent years it has become one of the best-selling sneakers on earth. There is nothing rare about a Samba, which is the point: the black-and-gum version here is the permanent one, and it will still look correct in a decade. Note this one sits in the 20%-off tier rather than 30%.

## DANTON's stand-collar workshirt — HK$1,299 (about US$167)

<figure>
  <img src="/images/iteshop-danton-shirt.jpg" alt="White cotton stand-collar workshirt with red diamond logo and slanted chest pockets" />
  <figcaption>The red diamond at the chest: DANTON's mark since 1935.</figcaption>
</figure>

DANTON was founded in France in 1935 as a workwear maker — sturdy cotton garments for labourers, railwaymen and the like, with a small red diamond stitched at the chest. The brand's modern chapter was written from Japan, where it was bought and rebuilt with obsessive attention to the original patterns, which is why today's DANTON feels like French workwear passed through Japanese quality control. This white stand-collar shirt carries the full vocabulary: the diamond logo, the curved chest pockets, the collar that stands without a button. It wears like a light jacket over a tee and, in the 30%-off tier, pairs neatly with the Samba above to trigger the discount.

## BEAUTY & YOUTH's double-breasted blazer — HK$1,499 (about US$192)

<figure>
  <img src="/images/iteshop-beauty-youth-blazer.jpg" alt="Grey-brown oversized double-breasted blazer with peak lapels" />
  <figcaption>Grey-brown, oversized, double-breasted — Japanese ametora applied to the blazer.</figcaption>
</figure>

BEAUTY & YOUTH belongs to UNITED ARROWS, one of the Tokyo select shops that, since the late eighties, has studied American casual dress with academic seriousness. The Japanese call this tradition ametora, and its output — relaxed shoulders, generous cuts, odd textures — now flows back westward and shows up in every brand's lookbook. This blazer is a clean example: oversized, double-breasted, in a grey-brown speckled weave that reads as linen but is built for autumn. It is the most expensive piece here, which also makes it the best use of the extra 5% — a blazer, a shirt, and the code 26D11 together clear the HK$1,500 line comfortably.

## Before the 12th

The sale closes on November 12, and the 26D11 code is quota-limited, first come first served, so the extra 5% is the part that can actually run out. One way to shop a sale like this: skip the pieces that only look good at a discount, and buy the discounted versions of things that were never going away anyway — a Samba, a striped tee, a plain black tote. Every piece above fits that description. The price is temporary; most of the designs are not.
