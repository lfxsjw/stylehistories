There is no object in an American closet that announces money so quietly as the penny loafer. It says "old family" without the family, "boarding school" without the school, "country club" without the club. It is the shoe that wealthy people wear to signal that they have never once had to think about shoes. And every bit of that signal is fake.

The penny loafer is a Norwegian farmer's work shoe. That is not a metaphor or a flourish. The design that G.H. Bass of Wilton, Maine turned into the most American shoe on earth was copied, almost line for line, from a slip-on moccasin that Norwegian farmers and fishermen wore to work in mud and on boats. The name Bass gave it, "Weejuns," is just the word "Norwegians" squashed into a playful American nickname. Strip away the horsehair polish and the campus nostalgia and you are left with a farm boot that somehow became the official footwear of pretending you were born rich.

That transformation, from barn to Ivy League, is the real story. And it runs through one of the strangest details in all of fashion history: a slot in the leather, cut to hold a single penny.

## A shoe invented somewhere else

The chain starts in Aurland, a small town in western Norway, on a fjord so narrow the mountains nearly touch. In the 1920s a local shoemaker named Nils Gregoriussen Tveranger made a slip-on shoe by combining two things he had seen: the soft moccasins of the indigenous people he had met on a trip to North America, and the practical, sturdy shoes his own neighbors wore on fishing boats. The result was a comfortable, laceless shoe with a raised seam stitched across the top of the foot. Locals called it, plainly, the Aurland shoe.

It was built to be cheap, tough, and easy to get on and off, which is exactly what you want when your hands are wet and the deck is moving. Nobody in Aurland thought of it as elegant. It was a work shoe, made for people who stood in boats all day.

What happened next is a small lesson in how fashion actually moves, which is rarely the way we imagine. British tourists and sportsmen, fishing in Norway between the wars, noticed the comfortable shoes the locals wore and brought pairs home. The shoe drifted to European resorts, then to America. In 1936, an editor at *Esquire* spotted a version of it in Palm Beach, Florida, and thought it had something. The magazine worked with Bass and a New York retailer called Rogers Peet to turn the Norwegian slip-on into an American product.

Bass did what American manufacturers always do: it took the foreign idea and improved the marketing. The company added a leather strip, a "saddle," stitched across the front of the shoe, with a diamond-shaped slit cut into it. That slit was pure ornament, a design flourish with no function. Then Bass gave the shoe its American name. Nobody at the time would have predicted that the name, and the pointless slit, would do more to sell the shoe than the shoe itself ever could.

## The penny nobody actually carried

Here is where the story gets good, because here is where it becomes legend and stops being fact.

The famous version, repeated in magazines and menswear blogs and brand histories alike, goes like this. In the 1950s, Ivy League students began tucking a penny into the slit on their loafers. They did it so they would always have a coin on hand for a payphone, in case of emergency. The habit gave the shoe its name, and the name stuck.

It is a lovely story. It is also, by every account I can find, almost certainly invented. Even G.H. Bass's own history, which has every incentive to repeat the romantic version, is careful to call it "popular lore" and note that "the origin of the habit is difficult to verify." A payphone call in 1950s America cost about ten cents, so the arithmetic roughly works. But no primary source from the period, no campus photograph caption or contemporaneous article, actually documents students doing this. The tale reads like a story told after the fact to explain a name that had already happened.

What is true is more interesting. The penny wasn't functional. It was a signal. A student who put a shiny coin in his loafer was advertising that he was the kind of person who had a penny to spare and nowhere to spend it. It was casual wealth, performed for an audience of other students who understood the code. The penny didn't buy a phone call. It bought status.

This is the part of the loafer's history nobody likes to sit with, because it spoils the romance. The penny loafer's entire identity, its name, its mystique, was built on a coin that served no purpose except to look like it belonged. The shoe wasn't the signal. The coin in the shoe was the signal. And the whole apparatus was aimed at one audience: young men at a handful of East Coast colleges who wanted everyone to know they came from money without ever having to say it.

## How a work shoe became a class uniform

The loafer landed on American campuses in the 1950s and 1960s, at exactly the moment the "Ivy League look" was hardening into a uniform. Chinos, oxford-cloth button-down shirts, tweed jackets, and loafers. The shoe's appeal was that it looked effortless, which is the whole trick of prep style: it is a costume built entirely out of the appearance of not being a costume.

John F. Kennedy wore them. Grace Kelly wore them. Paul Newman wore them. Audrey Hepburn wore a version in *Funny Face*. Miles Davis wore them so often that he picked up a nickname, "the Warlord of the Weejuns," which is about as cool a thing as a shoe has ever been called. By the 1960s the penny loafer had stopped being a shoe and become a membership card.

Then something predictable happened to a garment that had become a class uniform: the other classes wanted in, and they wore it wrong on purpose.

In Britain and the United States, the mods, the punks, and the skinheads all took the penny loafer, the shoe of the country club and the prep school, and wore it as aggression. A skinhead in a pair of polished loafers was not signaling wealth. He was signaling that he had taken your uniform and was now using it against you. This is the point at which the loafer's history gets genuinely interesting, because the shoe that was invented to announce "I don't have to work" was now being worn by working-class kids to announce "I see your costume and I reject it."

The loafer could hold both meanings at once because it was never really about the shoe. It was always about what the shoe pointed to: the people on the other side of the class line.

## Ninety years later, the lie still sells

This year, 2026, the penny loafer turns ninety. G.H. Bass launched the Weejun in 1936, which means the shoe is now officially an antique, and the brand has marked the anniversary with a collection that puts a commemorative coin in the slot, closing the loop on the myth the company itself helped invent. The timing is not a coincidence. The loafer is having one of its periodic revivals, and this one is loud enough that *The Times* declared 2026 "the year of the loafer" back in January.

The runways have followed. Miu Miu has pushed the "grandpa loafer," a deliberately aged, used-looking version. Prada has done a penny loafer in shiny patent leather. Gucci's horsebit loafer, the Italian luxury variant, keeps selling as a status object decades after Aldo Gucci first added the metal bit in 1953. Saint Laurent's "Le Loafer," with its wavy hand-stitched toe and the Cassandre logo, has become one of the most copied shoes on the internet. None of these shoes are for farmers. All of them are for signaling.

What I keep coming back to is how little the mechanics have changed in ninety years. A Norwegian shoemaker built a comfortable, practical shoe for working people. An American company copied it, added a pointless strap and a pointless slit, and sold it to rich kids as a symbol of exactly the ease that comes from never having to work in the first place. The penny was the final touch: a coin with no job, sitting in a slot with no function, on a shoe that had been stripped of its original purpose and repackaged as proof of leisure.

The penny loafer is, in the end, a very honest document of how American style works. It takes something real, something worn by people who actually stood in boats and got wet, and turns it into a fantasy of effortless wealth. Then it sells that fantasy back to everyone, rich and poor, for ninety years running.

I don't know how to feel about that, exactly. The shoe is comfortable. The story behind it is a little sad and a little funny and mostly American. If you own a pair of penny loafers, you are now, technically, wearing a Norwegian farm shoe that has been lying to people since 1936. Whether you put a penny in the slot is up to you. It never did anything anyway.
