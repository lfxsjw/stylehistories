The polo shirt is the most casually insulted garment in menswear. Call it basic. Call it the uniform of men who say "synergy." It has survived both, and it barely seems to notice. But the thing nobody remembers is that it was not invented for polo at all. The shirt you associate with horses, country clubs, and a certain kind of inherited confidence was dreamed up by a French tennis player who was sick of sweating through a starched collar.

Here is the stranger part. The man who created it never called it a polo shirt. He called it the L.12.12, which is about as romantic as a part number. The name that stuck came later, from a sport the shirt had nothing to do with. If you want to understand why the polo shirt has meant so many contradictory things over the last century, start there: it has been mislabeled from the day it left the factory.

## A man who couldn't stand his own shirt

René Lacoste was one of the best tennis players of the 1920s. He won seven Grand Slam singles titles and helped France take the Davis Cup back from the Americans in 1927, the first time a non-American team had held it in years. The French press called him and three teammates the "Four Musketeers." But what bothered him daily was simpler than all that. The standard tennis uniform of his era was a long-sleeved cotton shirt, starched stiff, worn with a tie and flannel trousers. On a hot American court it was a small, damp prison.

So Lacoste cut the sleeves off. In 1926, at the US Open, he showed up in a short-sleeved shirt he had commissioned for himself, made from a loosely knit cotton jersey called petit piqué. The fabric had a faint waffled texture that let air through and held its shape without starch. The collar stayed soft and flat. He won the tournament in it.

This should not have been a revolution. It was one fastidious man solving a private annoyance. But that is how a lot of clothing history actually works: someone gets uncomfortable enough to fix the problem, and the fix turns out to be exactly what everyone else wanted.

## The crocodile came before the brand

The logo deserves its own footnote, because it predates the company by several years and it happened almost by accident. In 1923, Lacoste made a bet with his Davis Cup captain, Allan Muhr, over a crocodile-skin suitcase in a Boston shop window. If Lacoste won a decisive match, the suitcase was his. He lost the match and the suitcase. But a journalist from the *Boston Evening Transcript* heard about the wager and started calling him "the Crocodile," partly for the suitcase and partly for the way he never let go of a point.

Lacoste liked it. In 1927 he asked his friend Robert George, a stylist, to embroider a crocodile on his white blazer. This is widely credited as the first time a logo appeared on the outside of a garment, a move that now seems so ordinary we forget anyone had to do it first. Before Lacoste, clothes did not advertise their maker. After him, that was the whole game.

## L.12.12, the unglamorous name

Lacoste retired in 1929, aged twenty-five, and went looking for something to do. In 1933 he teamed up with André Gillier, who ran one of France's largest knitwear firms, and started making the shirt commercially. They named it the L.12.12. The L was for Lacoste. The 1 was for petit piqué, the fabric. The 2 was for short sleeves. The 12 was because it was the twelfth prototype. It is hard to imagine a less marketing-savvy name, which tells you how little the two men were thinking about branding and how much they were thinking about getting the shirt right.

For the first decade or two the shirt came only in white, and only for men. It was a tennis shirt. It was sold as a tennis shirt. The crocodile on the chest was a detail, not yet a status signal.

## How it became "polo"

So where did the name come from? Not from Lacoste. The word "polo shirt" existed before Lacoste's shirt did, attached to a different garment. In 1896, Brooks Brothers introduced a button-down collar shirt inspired by the actual sport of polo, where players buttoned their collars down so they wouldn't flap in their faces at a gallop. For decades, "polo shirt" meant that: a long-sleeved, button-down dress shirt.

Then Ralph Lauren got hold of the term. In 1972 he launched his own short-sleeved cotton piqué shirt under his "Polo" label and called it the Polo shirt. The name caught on and swallowed the category. A tennis shirt invented by a Frenchman in 1926 was now, for most of the English-speaking world, a "polo shirt." The garment had been renamed after a sport it was never made for, by a brand built on a romantic idea of WASP aristocracy that the shirt's actual inventor had no part in.

That is the whole joke, and it explains a lot. The polo shirt is a tennis player's invention wearing an aristocrat's name. No wonder it has spent a century being pulled in opposite directions.

## The shirt that wouldn't stay classy

Because here is what actually happened to Lacoste's invention. It did not stay a country-club garment. It kept escaping into other people's hands.

In 1951 Lacoste finally made the shirt in colors, and a year later the American clothing maker David Crystal licensed it for the US market under the name Izod Lacoste. Through the 1980s, that little crocodile became a suburban status marker, the thing a certain kind of man wore to signal that he had made it. The preppy look ran with it. John F. Kennedy, Dwight Eisenhower, and the Duke of Windsor all wore the shirt, and it inherited some of their easy confidence.

Then the other end of the spectrum took it back. British football casuals in the 1980s adopted expensive sportswear labels as a kind of ironic uniform. Hip-hop picked up the tennis shirt and wore it loose and oversized. By the 2000s the polo had been through so many hands that a single garment could mean "I summer in the Hamptons" or "I'm in a music video" depending on who was wearing it and how.

I think that churn is exactly why the shirt survives. It is blank enough to take any meaning you load onto it, and recognizable enough that the meaning lands. A tennis player's small fix became the most adaptable top in menswear because it had no strong identity of its own to resist the next group that claimed it.

## What I actually make of it

The thing I keep coming back to is the misnomer. We call it a polo shirt, and the name does real work: it dresses the garment up in a sport of aristocrats and makes it sound like it was always theirs. But the actual history is a French champion muttering about his collar and cutting his sleeves off. The distance between those two stories is the whole appeal. The polo shirt lets you borrow the class signal without paying any of the dues.

Maybe that is why people make fun of it. The mockery is aimed at the name and the pretension, not the shirt. The shirt itself is a genuinely good piece of engineering: a collar that stays flat, a fabric that breathes, a cut you can move in. If Lacoste had named it after tennis, we would all still be wearing it and nobody would feel defensive about it.

So next time you see a crocodile on a chest, remember what it actually means. Not horses, not inherited wealth, not a country club. It means a man in 1926 got too hot in a starched shirt and decided to do something about it. Everything pretentious about the polo shirt was added later, by other people. The shirt underneath was just a good idea, and good ideas are hard to keep classy.
