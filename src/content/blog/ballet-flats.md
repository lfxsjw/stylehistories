Here is a sentence that should not make sense: the most comfortable shoe in most women's closets is named after a sport famous for destroying feet. A professional ballerina's pointe shoe is a hardened slipper that forces her body weight onto the very tips of her toes, and dancers have bled through them, broken toes in them, and retired young because of them. The ballet flat, the soft little slip-on that millions of women wear because it does not hurt, took its name from that same world. The flat is what happens when you strip away everything ballet did to a woman's foot and keep only the shape, and the shape turned out to be exactly what ordinary feet had been asking for all along.

The history of the flat shoe is long and not very glamorous, which is the point. Long before ballet existed, people wore soft, flat, simple shoes because that is what you wear when you have to walk places. In the sixteenth century men wore a flat shoe called a *pompe*, and for centuries after that, when a person had to actually move, flat was the default. Then heels arrived and ruined the sensible arrangement. Catherine de' Medici, legend has it, asked a cobbler to add height to her wedding shoes in the 1530s, and the heel became a marker of status that spread across Europe. It stuck around until Marie Antoinette, who supposedly walked to the guillotine in 1793 wearing a pair of heels, after which flat shoes came back in fashion as quickly as the monarchy fell.

The ballet connection is where it gets strange, because for a long time ballet dancers themselves wore heels. When the Royal Academy of Dance opened in Paris in 1680, ballerinas danced in the same heeled shoes as everyone else, fashion mattering more than balance. That held until the 1720s, when a dancer named Marie Camargo decided she wanted to actually move. Camargo was famous for her jumps and her quick footwork, the kind of thing that is hard to do in heels, so she shortened her skirt a few inches and switched to flat slippers. It worked. She became one of the most celebrated dancers of her era, and the flat slipper became the dancer's shoe from then on. The flat shoe did not start as fashion. It started as a woman refusing to let a shoe get in the way of her body.

The flat did not become a street shoe, though, until a series of women and one cobbler pulled it out of the theater. The cobbler was Salvatore Capezio, an Italian immigrant who had set up shop in New York and become the shoemaker to the Metropolitan Opera, making dance shoes for the people on stage. In the early 1940s, with wartime rationing making leather and other materials scarce, the American designer Claire McCardell asked Capezio to make her ballet-style flats with rubber soles and fabric uppers that matched her clothes. McCardell designed clothes for real life, for women who moved, and she saw in the dancer's shoe something her customers could walk in. Capezio agreed, and the modern ballet flat was born: a dancer's slipper with a sole sturdy enough for the sidewalk.

McCardell deserves more credit than she usually gets in this story. She was not chasing a trend. She was solving a specific problem, which was that women in the 1940s were being asked to choose between heels they could barely stand in and clunky practical shoes with no grace. The ballet flat was her answer to both. She showed them in 1953 at an exhibition of twenty years of her work at the Frank Perls Gallery in Beverly Hills, and the reaction told her she had hit something. A shoe that looked delicate but did not punish you. The idea was simple enough to feel obvious, which is usually the sign that it was not obvious at all.

The other half of the story is French, and it involves a mother who was not trying to make fashion at all. Rose Repetto was an Italian-born woman living in Paris whose son, Roland Petit, was a dancer and choreographer. The story, which Repetto told for the rest of her life, is that Roland came home from class one day in 1947 complaining that his feet hurt, and she sat down and hand-stitched him a softer shoe. It worked well enough that other dancers at the Paris Opera started asking for pairs, and soon a woman who had set out to help her son had a small shoemaking business. Repetto's real invention was a technique called *stitch-and-return*, sewing the shoe inside out and then flipping it, which made a seam that never rubbed the foot. Dancers are picky about this kind of thing, and the fact that Rudolf Nureyev and Mikhail Baryshnikov bought their shoes from her is the only endorsement that matters.

Then, in 1956, Brigitte Bardot walked in and asked for something nobody had thought to ask for. Bardot had trained as a dancer as a girl and knew what a soft ballet slipper felt like. She wanted a version of it she could wear on the street in a film she was about to shoot, *And God Created Woman*. Repetto made her a pair, a delicate flat with a small bow, and called it the Cendrillon, after Cinderella. The film made Bardot a star, and the shoes she wore in it became one of the first things women asked for by name. A year later, Audrey Hepburn wore ballet flats with cigarette pants in *Funny Face*, and the shoe had officially escaped the theater for good. What had started as a mother's favor to her son became, within a decade, one of the most recognizable shoes in the world.

There is a quiet irony in the Bardot detail that is worth sitting with. The ballet flat's most famous early wearer was a sex symbol, and the shoe was marketed hard on that association for decades. But the thing itself is the least sexual shoe imaginable. It is flat, it covers the foot, it makes no promise about the body beyond that its owner would like to be comfortable. Bardot wore it because she knew, from dancing, how good a soft slipper felt, and the world bought it for the same reason, even if it did not say so out loud.

The flat has never really gone away since, but it moves in cycles, and each cycle says something about what women were being asked to wear in between. It had a big run in the 1960s, when the pointed stiletto of the fifties finally exhausted everyone and a flat, round-toed shoe read as young and modern. It came back hard around 2005 to 2010, worn by Kate Moss and Sienna Miller and Mischa Barton, right as the chunky sneaker and the sky-high platform were demanding that feet suffer in the other direction. And it is back again now, in 2026, after several years of ugly-shoe dominance, ballet flats showing up on Jennifer Lawrence, Kendall Jenner, Margot Robbie, and a long list of others, in leather and mesh and Mary Jane straps and embellished versions of all kinds. Each time, the pattern is the same: the flat comes back exactly when women get tired of whatever uncomfortable thing fashion has been insisting on.

That is the real story of this shoe, and it is not about ballet at all. The ballet flat is named for a discipline that asks a woman to ignore her own pain, and it became, in ordinary life, the shoe a woman reaches for when she decides to stop ignoring it. Pointe shoes hurt on purpose, as the cost of an art form. Ballet flats hurt on purpose never. They are the shoe that says the day has been long enough. The name is a leftover, a bit of borrowed elegance, but the function is the most unglamorous and human thing there is: a soft shoe with a seam that does not rub, so you can walk home at the end of the day without thinking about your feet.

There is a lesson in how the flat keeps winning that fashion rarely admits. The industry treats comfort as a compromise, a thing you settle for when you cannot manage the real thing. The ballet flat is the counterargument, and it has been for eighty years. Claire McCardell did not settle when she put rubber soles on a dancer's slipper; she solved something. Rose Repetto did not compromise when she sewed a shoe inside out; she made a better object. And every few years, when women buy flats in the millions while the trend forecasters are busy explaining the next five-inch heel, they are making the same quiet choice those women made. Comfort is not the absence of style. It is a style, and it keeps coming back, because it works.

The next time you see a pair of ballet flats on a runway or in a store window, that is the whole history in a single object. A name borrowed from an art form that bleeds your feet, attached to a shoe whose only real purpose is to let you stop hurting. A dancer gave it its shape. A mother gave it its seam. And ordinary women, one pair at a time, gave it its real job.
