Paris, July 5, 1946. Four days earlier, the United States had detonated an atomic bomb above Bikini Atoll, a ring of coral in the Pacific, and the photographs were on every front page in the world. Now a French engineer named Louis Réard was standing beside a Paris swimming pool with thirty square inches of newspaper-print cloth and a problem: no model in Paris would agree to be seen in it.

So he hired a nineteen-year-old dancer from the Casino de Paris whose act involved wearing considerably less. Micheline Bernardini stepped out at the Piscine Molitor, smiled for the cameras, and held up a small box that the suit could be folded into. Réard, an automobile engineer who had taken over his mother's lingerie business a short walk from the pool, had already chosen the name. He called it the bikini.

## A stunt, not a design

The bikini was not born from a sketch in a fashion house. It was born from a marketing war between two Frenchmen, and the fight was over inches.

That spring, the couturier Jacques Heim had launched a two-piece he called the Atome and hired a skywriter to trail the words "the world's smallest bathing suit" over the beaches of the Riviera. Réard's answer was to cut his own suit smaller, down past the navel — the line no mainstream swimsuit had crossed — and to pick a louder name. The atomic tests at Bikini Atoll were the biggest news story on earth that week. Réard took the name outright, protected it, and advertised his suit as "smaller than the world's smallest bathing suit."

The debut itself was staged. Réard sponsored the Molitor's annual beauty contest to get a jury and a wall of photographers in one place, then had Bernardini parade before them. The photographs ran around the world; the International Herald Tribune alone ran nine stories, and Bernardini reportedly received some fifty thousand fan letters. Réard filed his patent fourteen days later.

It is worth pausing on how deliberate all of it was. The bikini was not a garment that drifted over a line of decency by accident. It was engineered to cross the line, with a name chosen for its shock value and a model chosen because she had nothing left to lose. Shock was the entire business plan.

## The Romans had it first

Here is the strange part: the two-piece was not new. It was, in fact, absurdly old.

On a hillside near Piazza Armerina, Sicily, the Villa Romana del Casale holds the largest collection of Roman mosaics in the world. In one room, a floor from the early fourth century AD shows ten young women working out in two-piece outfits: bandeau tops, briefs, anklets. They lift hand weights, throw the discus, run races, play a ball game. In the center, a winner holds a palm branch and crowns herself with roses.

Archaeologists, who enjoy being precise about these things, will tell you the garments are a strophium and a subligaculum — a chest band and a loincloth — and that this was athletic wear, not swimwear. Fine. The point is that sixteen centuries before Réard cut his triangles, women in the Roman Empire were exercising in public in garments that would pass without comment on any beach today, and the villa's owner saw nothing worth hiding.

The modern world had been shrinking its swimwear on its own, too. American wartime rationing rules ordered a ten percent cut in swimsuit fabric, and by the early 1940s Hollywood stars like Ava Gardner and Rita Hayworth were photographed in two-piece suits. The bikini's only real innovation was the navel. Hollywood's production code allowed two-pieces on screen but treated the belly button as contraband, and the wider culture drew its line in the same place.

## The bans

The establishment took the bait exactly as designed. Spain, Italy and Portugal banned the bikini from their beaches. Mayors on France's own Atlantic coast forbade it. The Vatican pronounced it sinful. In the United States, the swimsuit manufacturer Fred Cole told Time in 1950 that he had "little but scorn" for the French bikini, on the grounds that French girls had short legs. That is a real quote from a real swimsuit mogul, and it captures the level of argument on offer.

The pageant world produced the perfect scandal in 1951, when Sweden's Kiki Håkansson won the first Miss World contest wearing a bikini. The crown, awarded in a garment that would not raise an eyebrow at any pool today, was treated like a diplomatic incident, and several countries threatened to withdraw from future pageants over it.

Réard kept his shop and his devotees, and marketed both with a straight face. He converted a Packard into a car-cabin-cruiser hybrid, portholes and cockpit and all, and drove it from beach to beach with young women aboard, causing traffic jams of rubberneckers, for the next three decades. But for most of the 1950s the bikini was more famous than worn.

## How it actually won

What changed was not a designer and not an argument. It was repetition, by people who had stopped asking.

Brigitte Bardot, eighteen years old, let photographers catch her in a bikini at Cannes in 1953 until the pictures made her the face of the garment, and she kept wearing one in her films until *And God Created Woman* (1956) made both of them famous. She treated the suit as ordinary. Ordinariness was the whole strategy.

In the summer of 1960, a novelty song called "Itsy Bitsy Teenie Weenie Yellow Polka Dot Bikini" reached number one in America. It is a song about a girl so shy about her new swimsuit that she hides in the water rather than be seen in it, which is a strange anthem for a breakthrough. Teenagers heard it and went out and bought the thing anyway; that summer, retailers could hardly keep bikinis in stock.

Two years later, Ursula Andress walked out of the Caribbean surf in *Dr. No* wearing a white bikini with a belt for a knife, and cinema settled the matter. The suit she wore sold at auction in 2001 for about £35,000. In 1964, Sports Illustrated printed its first Swimsuit Issue. By the middle of the sixties, the bikini had gone from contraband to cliché in under twenty years.

Notice what did the work. Nobody approved it. Nobody defended it in print. It kept showing up — on an eighteen-year-old at Cannes, in a pop song, on a Bond girl — until showing up was normal. A shopkeeper gambled that manufactured scandal could be converted into legitimacy, and it worked, though it took fifteen years and cost him nothing but nerve.

## The matchbox

Réard ran his boutique for close to forty years, retired around 1980, and died in 1984, by which point the garment he had named after a bomb was so ordinary that supermarkets sold it in three-packs.

And somewhere in Sicily, the ten girls of the Villa Romana del Casale are still running their races on a fourth-century floor, sixteen centuries deep into the pause that Réard briefly interrupted. They lift their weights, toss their ball, crown their winner. If they know anything about the bans, the Vatican, and the swimsuit mogul who worried about French legs, they are not showing it.

It was always just clothes.
