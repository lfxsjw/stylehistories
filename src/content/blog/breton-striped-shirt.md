Count the stripes on a real Breton shirt and you will find twenty-one. That number is not a design choice. It is a safety measure, written into a regulation of the French Navy dated 27 March 1858, which set down exactly how the thing should be made: twenty or twenty-one indigo stripes on the body, each one half as wide as the white band beside it, fourteen on the sleeves. The point was not elegance. Sailors told each other the stripes made a man easier to see if he went overboard, a bright pattern breaking the flat grey of the sea.

No one in 1858 imagined this shirt would end up in the closet of a fashion editor. It was workwear, issued to quartermasters and seamen from Brittany, which is how it earned the name "Breton." Yet a century and a half later, a striped knit top is still the fastest shorthand for "French person" in a costume box. That is the odd part of this story. The shirt never tried to be fashionable. It just kept getting found.

## A uniform that outlived the job

The marinière, as the French call it, was regulation kit. The 1858 decree specified a cotton jersey knit, a boat neck that reached the throat, and sleeves no longer than the over-jacket — three-quarter length, in practice. The stripes were not decoration. On a grey Atlantic swell, a drowned man in a striped shirt was a man you might actually locate. The logic was grim and practical, and it worked well enough that the design stayed essentially unchanged for generations.

The Breton connection mattered. The French Navy drew heavily on Brittany, a coastal region with its own fishing and maritime culture, and sailors from there brought the shirt into port towns. Over the late nineteenth century it stopped being purely naval. Fishermen wore it. Dock workers wore it. It was cheap, warm, and dried fast, which is more than most clothing could promise in 1880.

Two French knitwear companies, Saint James and Armor-Lux, began producing the striped jersey for civilians, and Saint James still supplies the French Navy today. By the end of the nineteenth century, striped knits had crossed into swimwear across Europe. The shirt was already spreading beyond the sea before any designer noticed it.

## The Chanel myth, mostly

Here is where the standard telling goes wrong. The version most people know says Coco Chanel saw sailors in striped shirts on holiday in Deauville, copied them, and turned the marinière into fashion single-handedly around 1917. There is a famous photograph of Chanel wearing one in 1928, and the story has been repeated so often it has hardened into fact.

The textile historian Lourdes Font has picked at this. Font's argument, laid out in interviews and her work on Chanel, is that the stripes themselves were already everywhere. By the 1920s, modernism loved a clean graphic line, and plenty of designers were using stripes. Chanel was not alone in noticing them. What she actually borrowed, Font says, was the sailor collar — and what she actually changed was the cut, loosening the garment so it hung off the body instead of pinning it with a corset. That loose, comfortable shape, in soft jersey rather than stiff fabric, was the real departure.

None of this makes Chanel's influence small. It just moves it. The marinière in Chanel's hands stopped being a uniform and became leisurewear for women who wanted to move freely. She did not invent the stripes; she invented the idea that a woman could wear a sailor's working shirt and look at ease rather than slumming it. The distinction sounds fussy until you realize it is the difference between "Chanel discovered this garment" and "this garment was already so good that Chanel, like everyone else, reached for it."

## Picasso, the Murphys, and a shirt that traveled

The people who actually pushed the stripe into civilian fashion were not always designers. The American couple Gerald and Sara Murphy moved to the French Riviera in the early 1920s and befriended everyone — the Fitzgeralds, Ernest Hemingway, Dorothy Parker, Pablo Picasso. Gerald took a trip to Marseille for boat supplies and came back with a stack of striped sailor shirts, which he handed out to his guests. Photographs from that circle show the stripe spreading through the smartest party on the coast.

Picasso was rarely photographed out of one after the 1940s, and he painted himself wearing a striped shirt in a 1943 self-portrait. Brigitte Bardot made it a sex symbol in the 1950s, photographed in Saint-Tropez in little more than a striped top and a pair of capri pants. Jean Seberg wore one in *Breathless* (1960), which stamped the shirt onto the French New Wave. Marcel Marceau wore one on stage. So did John Wayne. So did, decades later, Kurt Cobain and the Duchess of Cambridge, for entirely different reasons.

Then Jean Paul Gaultier took the stripe and ran with it from the 1980s onward, building a whole visual identity out of the sailor motif — the stripes on his perfume bottles, the striped crews on his runway staff, the marinière as his personal uniform. Gaultier did not resurrect the shirt. He just made explicit something that had been true for a century: the stripe had become its own language, independent of any navy.

## Why it refuses to date

Most uniforms become costumes the moment they leave service. A sailor suit is a children's outfit now. The Breton stripe did the opposite. It shed its context and kept its authority.

Part of it is the geometry. Horizontal stripes are simple enough to survive any decade's reinterpretation, and the navy-on-white contrast works in almost any wardrobe. But I think the deeper reason is that the shirt carries a story that is still legible. Wear it and you are borrowing from workers, from the sea, from the Riviera, from French cinema — a whole pile of references that add up to something specific and hard to fake. A striped top says "I know where this comes from" even when the wearer has never seen the sea.

There is also the democracy of it. A real marinière from Saint James costs the same whether a fisherman or a fashion editor buys it. The same object spans the whole range. That is rare in clothing, where price usually sorts people into clean lines. The stripe refuses to cooperate.

The shirt has not changed much since 1858. The neck is still a boat, the sleeves still three-quarter, the stripes still twenty-one on a traditional cut. Saint James still knits them. What changed is everything around it, which is exactly how a garment becomes permanent: it stays put while the world rearranges itself around it.

## What I actually notice now

I have a marinière that I bought years ago, and I still reach for it more than almost anything else. Not because it is fashionable. Because it is never wrong. It goes under a blazer without apology and over a swimsuit without irony.

That is the trick of the whole story, and I keep circling back to it. The marinière was designed to keep a drowning man visible. Instead it became the most recognizable piece of "French style" in the world, a garment that generations of designers, painters, and actors kept picking up without anyone ever needing to explain why. Nothing about it was planned. It just survived, because it was good at its job, and it turned out the job could be anything.

Next time you see a striped shirt, count the stripes. Twenty-one, if it is the real thing. A number from a drowning regulation, a century and a half ago, now worn to the supermarket without a second thought. That is the whole achievement, and nobody gets the credit.
