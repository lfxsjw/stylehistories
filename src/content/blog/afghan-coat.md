If you've been anywhere near a vintage shop or a Depop feed this winter, you've seen it: the shearling coat with the big fur collar, the embroidery running down the sleeves, the way it makes whoever wears it look like they just walked off the set of a seventies road movie. The listings call it a "Penny Lane coat," after Kate Hudson's character in *Almost Famous*. The name has stuck so completely that most people never learn the coat had another name first.

It was called the Afghan coat. And the women who made it never got their name on it.

The coat people now associate with a fictional groupie began as winter survival gear in Ghazni province, the high, cold country between Kabul and Kandahar. Sheepskins were cured and tanned — men did that work, turning the hides a particular yellow using the rinds of pomegranates — then cut and sewn with the fleece on the inside and the soft leather on the outside. Women and girls did the part that made the coats unmistakable: they embroidered the yellow suede with flowers and geometric shapes in red and yellow silk thread, covering the front panels and sleeves with stitch after stitch. Each coat was unique, because each one was made by hand, by a specific person whose name we will never know.

The embroidery wasn't decoration for decoration's sake. In a country where winters are brutal, the coats had to be warm first, but the stitching carried meaning too — motifs passed down through generations, patterns that varied by region, sometimes marking social standing. This was national dress. Women wore them as often as men. A British archaeologist named Sylvia Matheson passed through Ghazni in 1955 and found "one shop after another offering nothing but pustin," as the coats were called. She bought a short one, a *pustincha*, and wrote that it was "the yellow skin entirely covered in closely stitched flowers of pillar-box red."

Then the hippies found it.

In the early 1960s, Afghanistan began a push toward modernization, and Western travelers started pouring down what became known as the Hippie Trail, overland from Istanbul through Kabul toward Kathmandu. They landed on a street in Kabul called Chicken Street — once full of poultry vendors, by then a strip of antique and carpet dealers that the first Lonely Planet guide, published in 1973, would call "the freak centre of Kabul." Some of those travelers weren't just shopping; they were scouting inventory. A young American named Craig Sams passed through Kabul in 1965, settled in London, and started importing the coats. His main outlet was Granny Takes a Trip, the wildest boutique on the King's Road in Chelsea.

That's where it turned from a souvenir into a rock-and-roll uniform. The Beatles visited the shop and walked out wearing the coats. In May 1967, at the London launch of *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band*, John Lennon turned up in an Afghan coat — tanned yellow, embroidered with big red flowers down the front and sleeves, worn with the fur inside and the skin out. Jimi Hendrix wore one. David Bowie wore one to his wedding. Jane Birkin wore one. Demand exploded so fast that the artisans in Ghazni couldn't keep up, and production moved to bigger workshops in Kabul, where the embroidery became faster and rougher. By 1969, the historian Tim Bonyhady has written, more of these coats were being worn outside Afghanistan than inside it.

Here's the part I keep turning over. The coat's entire appeal in the West was that it was handmade — the visible stitch, the one-of-a-kind embroidery, the sense that no machine had touched it. Hippies bought it precisely because it rejected mass production. And yet the very act of wanting it so badly destroyed the thing they wanted it for. The demand the counterculture created is what pushed the craft out of Ghazni's homes and into Kabul's sweatshops, where the embroidery got faster, cheaper, and less careful. A garment that had been a way for women to show their skill became a commodity to be cranked out for tourists. The hippies wanted authenticity, and their appetite for it is part of what made the authentic version scarce.

The coat faded when punk arrived in the eighties, then came roaring back around the turn of the millennium — Britney Spears, Lucy Liu, Sofia Coppola all wore them. But the real rebrand happened in 2000, with Cameron Crowe's *Almost Famous*. Kate Hudson's Penny Lane, a teenage groupie with a fur-trimmed coat and a knowing smile, became the face of the garment overnight. The name "Penny Lane coat" took over so completely that a thing with centuries of Afghan history behind it is now filed under a fictional character who, in the film, is nineteen years old and mostly in it for the music.

I don't think anyone who says "Penny Lane coat" means any harm. The name is just shorter, catchier, and easier to sell. But the rename does a specific kind of damage: it tells you that the only history worth keeping is the part that happened in the West, in front of a camera. The Ghazni women who stitched those flowers — the reason the coat looks the way it does — drop out of the picture entirely. What's left is a rock-and-roll prop.

What gets me is how little has changed. The coat is having another moment right now, driven by the same two engines as always: vintage resale and famous women. Bella Hadid buys vintage Afghan coats through Depop. Kendall Jenner has been photographed in a Connor Ives fur-and-silk robe with a clear Afghan-coat lineage. Designers like Charlotte Simone and Saks Potts make high-end versions in faux fur, priced like luxury and no longer tied to Afghanistan at all — you can buy the look without ever learning where it came from. Depop and Vinted are full of them, anywhere from £80 to £800 for the ornate old ones.

That's the strange ending to this story. The Afghan coat has been more popular outside Afghanistan than inside it for over half a century, and its current revival is no different. A garment made to keep mountain shepherds warm in a place most of its current owners will never visit is now a fashion statement whose name belongs to a movie. The embroidery is still the point — the flowers, the color, the handmade unevenness — but almost nobody wearing it now knows that those stitches were once a woman's signature, made in a yellow hide tanned with pomegranate rind, in a province most of us couldn't find on a map.

So here's the small thing I'd suggest. Next time you see one of these coats — on a stranger, on Instagram, on a rack — call it by its first name. Not because the name matters more than the coat, but because it's the least the women who made the originals are owed. You can't put their names on the tag anymore. But you don't have to keep erasing them either.
