There is a kind of clothing that has stopped meaning what it was built to mean, and nothing wears that contradiction quite like the hussar jacket. Every detail on it once did a job. The gold braid was not decoration but a way to spot rank through cannon smoke. The short cut kept it clear of a saddle. The fur-lined pelisse slung over one shoulder could stop a glancing sabre blow. Then, somewhere between the battlefield and the ballet stage, all of that function quietly evaporated, and what was left was pure signal. A jacket that screams before you open your mouth.

That is why it keeps coming back. Right now the hussar jacket is the loudest garment in fashion. In September, Seán McGirr opened McQueen's spring-summer 2026 show with a white military jacket so abbreviated it read as a swimsuit. Jonathan Anderson built his debut Dior menswear collection around the silhouette. Jenna Ortega turned up to Anderson's Dior womenswear show in a sleeveless black vest covered in gold frogging, and the internet did not stop posting it for weeks. Fashion data account @styleanalytics reported that global searches for "hussar jacket" jumped 300 percent in October alone. TikTok calls the whole thing "nutcracker-core," which tells you exactly how far the garment has travelled from its origins.

Those origins sit in Hungary in the fifteenth century. The word *hussar* most likely comes from the Hungarian *húsz*, meaning twenty: one tradition holds that King Matthias Corvinus decreed one man in twenty from each village should serve as a mounted raider against the Ottomans. These first hussars were light cavalry, fast and lightly armed, raised to scout and harry rather than to stand in a line. What they wore evolved from steppe riding coats, short jackets adapted from Turkish and Tartar garments, often slung over one shoulder so the sword arm stayed free. That slung coat was the seed of everything after it.

By the eighteenth century, the uniform had hardened into the shape we recognize. The fitted jacket, called a dolman, carried rows of horizontal braid across the chest, each ending in a barrel-shaped button called an olivet. A second, fur-trimmed jacket, the pelisse, hung over the left shoulder. A fur busby sat on the head. It was absurdly impractical and entirely deliberate. Frederick the Great recruited Hungarian cavalrymen wholesale into the Prussian army. Louis XIV founded the first French hussar regiment in 1692. Napoleon, who allegedly joked that a hussar would be lucky to live past thirty, surrounded himself with them. The jacket travelled because it projected something armies wanted their enemies to see: speed, dash, and a willingness to get killed in style.

Here is the part that matters for fashion, and it is easy to miss. The braid was never for beauty. On a smoky battlefield, an officer had to be identifiable at a distance, and a man with gold loops across his chest could be read a hundred yards out through the haze. The pelisse over the shoulder was not a cape but padding. The shako, the braided cuff, the standing collar, all of it was information. And yet the moment you strip away the context that made that information necessary, the information becomes ornament. The hussar jacket did not so much cross over into fashion as lose its reasons, one war at a time.

The nineteenth century finished the job. When breech-loading rifles and machine guns made cavalry obsolete, the hussar uniform became ceremonial dress, a costume for parades. It also became a costume for the stage. The toy soldiers in Tchaikovsky's *The Nutcracker* ballet, first performed in 1892, are dressed in miniature hussar jackets, and it is that image, more than any battle, that most people now associate with the silhouette. A garment built for intimidation ended up on a Christmas ornament. There is something almost tender about that fall.

Rock and roll found it next. Jimi Hendrix, who served briefly in the US Army before being discharged, filled his late-sixties wardrobe with antique hussar pelisses, wearing them unbuttoned over bare skin, draped in necklaces. The London National Army Museum identifies a famous 1967 Gered Mankowitz portrait of Hendrix as showing a jacket based on a British hussar pelisse. He kept the braid and the buttons and threw away everything else: the discipline, the rank, the obedience. That is the exact inversion the jacket was made for. The Beatles wore candy-colored satin guard coats on the cover of *Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band* in 1967, four fanciful colonels on the side of peace and love. Glam rockers from David Bowie to Marc Bolan to Queen took the military jacket and made it flamboyance itself. A garment designed to enforce order became the uniform of people trying to break it.

That cycle, order turned into noise, is why the jacket refuses to die. In 2005 Kate Moss wore a cream military jacket with tiny shorts and festival boots to Glastonbury, and the image became a defining photograph of the indie-sleaze era. A few years later, Christophe Decarnin at Balmain pushed decorated officer jackets into high-glam territory, worn by Beyoncé, Rihanna, and Michael Jackson, until they became a status symbol of the late 2000s. Every revival is the same gesture: take the authority, keep the shine, drop the obedience.

What is different this time is that the revival has gone fully democratic. You can buy a braided military jacket on Zara for around ninety dollars, or hunt a vintage one on Vinted, or, if you are patient, make one by sewing braid and buttons onto an old blazer. The current versions are not faithful reproductions. McGirr's are cropped and deconstructed. Ortega's is a vest. The trend has been styled back to the most casual possible base: faded jeans, a plain T-shirt, slouchy low-rise denim, a denim miniskirt. The rule everyone seems to have agreed on is that the more elaborate the jacket, the plainer everything under it should be. Ornament against nothing, on purpose.

That is what the hussar jacket actually is now: ornament with a memory. It remembers being built to intimidate, and it remembers being worn to rebel, and it holds both of those things at once without choosing. Most clothing does not get to be this many things across this many centuries. A white T-shirt means what it means and stops. The hussar jacket never stops meaning, which is precisely why it keeps getting dragged back out of the wardrobe every decade or so, dusted off, and pointed at a camera.

The next time you see a gold-braided jacket on a runway or on someone walking past you, remember that it started as a warning. The braid was there so a man could be seen, and counted, and feared, through cannon smoke. Now it is there so a person can be seen, full stop. Same signal, different war.
