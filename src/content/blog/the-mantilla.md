There is a painting in New York that shows a woman pointing at the sand. She wears black, with a mantilla of black lace over her curls. The words scratched into the sand read "Solo Goya" — only Goya.

It sounds like the end of a love affair, and it probably was. Francisco Goya's 1797 portrait of the 13th Duchess of Alba has been read for two centuries as proof of a romance between the painter and the most scandalous noblewoman in Spain. Whatever the truth, the image does something stranger than confess. The duchess is the wealthiest woman in the country, and she is dressed in the garment that was supposed to make women modest, invisible, obedient. Instead she looks directly at you and points to the only name she cares about. The veil is supposed to hide her. It makes her the center of the room.

That contradiction is the whole history of the mantilla in one painting. No other garment in fashion has been expected to mean so many opposite things at once, and survived precisely because it refuses to settle on any of them.

## A veil that never meant one thing

The mantilla — a lightweight lace or silk veil worn over the head and shoulders, usually lifted by a tall tortoiseshell comb called a peineta — appeared in the warmer regions of Spain near the end of the 16th century. It was a Spanish adaptation of the Christian practice of women covering their heads in prayer, and it spread through the Spanish empire to the Americas the way Spanish Catholicism did. By the 17th and 18th centuries, lace versions were showing up in the portraits of Velázquez and Goya.

That sounds like a simple story: a pious garment, rooted in church rules about modesty. It is not. Because almost from the start, the mantilla carried a second meaning that sat awkwardly next to the first.

Queen Isabella II made the mantilla fashionable in the 19th century, and the practice thinned out after her abdication in 1870. By 1900 it had retreated mostly to church, bullfights, Holy Week processions, and weddings. It became, in the popular imagination, shorthand for a conservative Spain — the Spain of black lace at Mass, of widows and processions. And it stayed that way through the Franco years, when it served as a visual marker of the regime's idea of Spanish womanhood: covered, Catholic, traditional.

But the same garment had always had a different reputation. Lace over the face does not merely hide a woman. It frames her. And for generations, women understood exactly what to do with that.

## The duchess who used modesty as a disguise

Here is what the curators of a new exhibition keep coming back to: the veil gave women anonymity, and anonymity gave women freedom.

The exhibition, "Mantilla: Fashion Unveiled," opened at the Hispanic Society Museum & Library in New York on November 20, 2026, and runs through March 28, 2027. It is the first major museum show devoted to the mantilla, curated by the Spanish-born scholar Carlos Usabiaga López. His argument is direct. "The understanding of the mantilla in all its complexity is often limited," he told ELLE, "either by a superficial reading of the symbol itself or by its association with an old-fashioned, rather conservative image of Spain."

The show's counter-reading starts with that Duchess of Alba. In Spain the veil was tied to religion and to a patriarchal idea of covering the female body. But, as Usabiaga put it to Vogue España, the chronicles of the period tell of women "like the Duchess of Alba" who used that very gesture "as a weapon of freedom." Anonymity, he says, "allowed them to do whatever they liked." A woman whose face was half-hidden could move through the world without being recognized, which for a duchess meant without being watched.

That is the detail that flips the whole garment on its head. The mantilla was sold as modesty and used as a disguise. The same lace that signaled submission in one room signaled escape in another.

## From piety to seduction to rebellion

The tension never resolved; it just kept finding new carriers.

At the turn of the twentieth century, Americans fell into what historians call the "Spanish craze," a fascination with Spain that ran roughly from 1890 to 1930. The mantilla became the visual shorthand for the whole country. The Spanish singer Raquel Meller landed on the cover of Time in 1926. Marlene Dietrich wore a mantilla in the 1935 film "The Devil Is a Woman," a title that says the quiet part out loud: the veil that signified Catholic modesty was, on a screen, pure seduction.

Then came the reinvention. In the 1980s, the countercultural movement known as La Movida Madrileña — the explosion of music, film, and art that followed Franco's death — grabbed the mantilla and pulled it in the opposite direction. Drag performers wore it. Artists turned the most conservative garment in Spain into a prop for provocation. The mantilla, a symbol of the regime's version of femininity, became one of the tools used to dismantle it.

Usabiaga points to exactly this reversal as the point of the exhibition: "From Spanish countercultural movements such as La Movida Madrileña to drag performance, the mantilla has repeatedly been appropriated and reimagined in ways that reveal its capacity to carry new meanings." A garment with no fixed meaning can be turned against whoever last tried to fix it.

The present day keeps the pattern going. Madonna has worn it. Rosalía wore a modernist take on it to the 2024 Met Gala. Dolce & Gabbana, whose design language leans heavily on Sicilian Catholic imagery, has borrowed it too. Cristóbal Balenciaga and Óscar de la Renta both drew on it long before. Designers return to the mantilla for the same reason Goya did: it gives them a face half in shadow, which is always more interesting than a face fully lit.

## What actually makes it work

Strip away the symbolism and the mantilla survives on a simpler trick. It is the only veil in Western fashion designed to be held away from the face.

The peineta, the tall tortoiseshell comb that lifts the lace off the crown of the head, is a 19th-century invention. Before it, a mantilla draped close over the hair like any chapel veil. The comb changed everything. It pushed the lace up and back, creating a frame around the face instead of a curtain over it. The woman wearing it was still covered — the rules were observed — but her face was now the center of the composition. Modesty, technically complied with, had been reengineered into a display.

That is the quiet genius of the garment, and the reason no one has ever successfully pinned a single meaning to it. It obeys the letter of the covering rule while completely undoing its purpose. It is a head covering that draws the eye to the head it covers.

Usabiaga has a line about the Seville icon of the Virgen de la Macarena, which came to be dressed in the actual mantilla of the actress and singer Juanita Reina, a devotee. "The same garment that serves that more erotic charge now dresses the Catholic, Apostolic and Roman Virgin," he said. "That versatility is very interesting." It is more than interesting. It is the entire story.

## The thing that keeps it alive

Most garments with a fixed meaning eventually go out of style, because meanings go out of style. The mantilla has outlasted almost all of them by refusing to have one.

A beret means France. A kilt means Scotland. These are costumes now, worn for effect or for ceremony. The mantilla has been through more identities than most countries have — modesty, status, seduction, national pride, Franco-era conservatism, queer rebellion, red-carpet drama — and it is still in active rotation because each new generation gets to pick which of those meanings it wants, or invent a new one.

The exhibition is called "Fashion Unveiled," which is a mild joke. The one thing the mantilla has never done is reveal a single, settled truth about itself. It has spent four hundred years hiding its meaning in plain sight, and it shows no sign of stopping.

That duchess in the painting pointed at the sand and wrote only one name. She didn't write her own. She didn't need to. She was wearing the one garment in the room that meant whatever she decided it meant that day, and she knew it.
