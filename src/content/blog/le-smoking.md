In the fall of 1966, Yves Saint Laurent put a woman in a tuxedo and his own customers walked away from it. The tuxedo was Le Smoking, the first dinner suit designed by a major couturier specifically for a woman's body, and at the haute couture level it sold exactly one. One. The same clients who paid thousands for his evening dresses looked at the most important thing he ever made and decided they did not want it. Sixty years later, in 2026, Saint Laurent opened and closed its fall runway show with a procession of tuxedos to mark the anniversary, and nobody at that show would have guessed that the garment began as a flop. The most radical thing a designer ever handed to women was, for a brief and telling moment, unwanted.

To understand why it failed, you have to understand what a tuxedo was for. The word itself is a leftover from a room most of us will never set foot in. In the nineteenth century, a gentleman retiring to the smoking room after dinner would put on a loose, comfortable jacket to keep the smell of cigar smoke off his good clothes. An 1865 order for a midnight-blue silk smoking jacket with matching trousers, placed by the Prince of Wales, is usually credited with nudging the garment toward evening wear. The tuxedo was a man's object, tied to men's spaces and men's rituals, and it carried that meaning the way a uniform does. Putting a woman in one was not a styling choice. It was a claim on a room she was not supposed to be in.

There had been glimpses before. Marlene Dietrich wore a man's tailcoat and top hat in *Morocco* in 1930 and the image shocked audiences precisely because it was a costume, a woman borrowing a man's clothes for effect. But Saint Laurent was not designing a costume. He was designing a garment, cut to a woman's body, meant to be worn on the street and at dinner and to work. He had been moving toward this for years. The androgynous style of his model and muse Danielle Luquet de Saint Germain and the evening looks of the artist Niki de Saint-Phalle both fed into it. The New York Times, reviewing the collection in August 1966, put it plainly: Saint Laurent had "copied" the black-tie trouser suit in velvet and wool. What he actually did was subtler and harder. He kept the dinner jacket's satin lapels and its authority, softened the collar, narrowed the waist, and cut the trousers to lengthen the leg. The result was not a woman disguised as a man. It was a woman wearing power that had been sized for her.

And the couture clientele said no. The Musée Yves Saint Laurent Paris, which is not in the habit of underselling its founder, states it without drama: "only one was sold." The house's own customers, the women Saint Laurent dressed for galas and weddings, took one look at the most famous garment he ever produced and bought a single example. This is the part of the story that usually gets skipped, because it does not fit the tidy narrative of a visionary ahead of his time. But it matters. The revolution did not start with the rich women it was first offered to. It started somewhere else entirely.

That somewhere else was a boutique on the Left Bank. In September 1966, Saint Laurent opened Rive Gauche, his ready-to-wear line, at 21 rue de Tournon in Paris. He was the first couturier to open a shop under his own name selling clothes you did not have to be measured for, and the store became, in the words of one museum curator, "a laboratory." The tuxedo that the couture salon had ignored was restaged there, priced around a hundred dollars, and the reaction was the opposite of the salon's. Customers waited in line for hours to buy it. The younger women Saint Laurent could not reach through couture found him through a shop window, and they understood the tuxedo in a way his established clients had not. For them it was not an affront to an evening dress code. It was a door.

What that door opened is hard to overstate, because in 1966 a woman in trousers was not simply unfashionable. She was, in France, technically in violation of a law that dated to 1800 and required women who wanted to wear pants to ask the police for permission. The ban was not formally struck down until 2013, which tells you how long the taboo outlived its usefulness. In New York, the socialite Nan Kempner was turned away from the restaurant La Côte Basque for arriving in her Saint Laurent trousers, whereupon she took the trousers off, walked in wearing the jacket as a minidress, and sat down. It is the single best thing anyone has ever done in a tuxedo, and it says everything about why the garment worked. It could not be kept out by a dress code, because the woman wearing it had already stopped asking permission.

Then the images started. Helmut Newton photographed the tuxedo for French Vogue in 1975 in a narrow Paris street, a model with slicked-back hair standing in the middle of the road in the suit, one hand in a pocket, a cigarette in the other. The picture became what is routinely called the most famous fashion photograph ever taken, and it fixed the tuxedo's meaning: a woman alone, composed, indifferent to whether she was being watched. Bianca Jagger married Mick Jagger in 1971 wearing a white Saint Laurent suit with nothing underneath the jacket, and the wedding photograph did for the suit what a hundred fashion editorials could not. Catherine Deneuve wore it, Lauren Bacall wore it, Liza Minnelli wore it. Saint Laurent put a smoking in every collection he designed until he retired in 2002. The garment that could not sell one at couture became the house's signature for four decades.

What the tuxedo actually did for women is worth being precise about, because "power dressing" has become such a drained phrase. It was not that a tailored jacket magically conferred authority. It was that it gave women the option of refusing a specific kind of attention. The evening dress asks to be looked at. The tuxedo asks to be addressed. When Kempner walked into La Côte Basque in a jacket and bare legs, she was not trying to be provocative; she was insisting on being treated as a person who had somewhere to be. That is the difference Saint Laurent understood, and it is why the tuxedo outlasted every hemline and silhouette that has come and gone since. Deneuve put it better than any critic: it changes your gestures. You carry yourself differently in a room when your clothes stop apologizing.

Sixty years on, the garment has not faded so much as become invisible, which is what happens to ideas that win. A woman in a black trouser suit is now so ordinary that we have to be reminded she was once illegal. Anthony Vaccarello, Saint Laurent's current creative director, marked the anniversary in 2026 by stripping the tuxedo down to its bones, opening his fall show with eight of them worn over bare skin and sending out six more before the end. The gesture reads as nostalgia only if you ignore what it is saying, which is that the house still thinks the tuxedo is its sharpest argument. It is not a revival. It is a reminder that this is where the whole conversation started.

Here is the thing I keep coming back to. Le Smoking is remembered as a stroke of genius, and it was. But the genius was not the design. The design was a men's jacket with the proportions fixed. The genius was in what Saint Laurent did when his own customers rejected it: he did not soften it, did not add a skirt, did not apologize and fold it back into the collection as a curiosity. He took it to a different audience and let them decide. The women who bought it from a Left Bank shop at a hundred dollars a suit were not the arbiters of fashion. They were just women who wanted to walk into a room and be taken seriously, and they turned a failed couture piece into the most important garment in women's fashion history. The tuxedo did not succeed because fashion finally came around to it. It succeeded because enough women stopped waiting for permission, and the industry spent the next sixty years trying to catch up.
